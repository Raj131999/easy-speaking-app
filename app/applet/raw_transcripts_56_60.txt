## 56. THE POWER OF VULNERABILITY | BRENÉ BROWN | TED

So, I'll start with this: a couple years ago, an event planner called me because I was going to do a speaking event. And she called, and she said, "I'm really struggling with how to write about you on the flyer." And I thought, "Well, what's the struggle?" And she said, "Well, I saw you speak, and I'm going to call you a researcher, I think, but I'm afraid if I call you a researcher, no one will come, because they'll think you're boring and irrelevant." (Laughter) And I was like, "Okay." And she said, "But the thing I liked about your talk is you're a storyteller. So I think I'll just call you a storyteller." And of course the academic, insecure part of me was like, "You're going to call me what?" And she said, "I'm going to call you a researcher-storyteller." And I was like, "Why not qualitative researcher?" (Laughter) But it's true: stories are data with a soul. And that's what I do: I study human connection. When you ask people about love, they tell you about heartbreak. When you ask people about belonging, they'll tell you their most excruciating experiences of being excluded. And when you ask people about connection, the stories they told me were about disconnection. So six years into my research, I stumbled upon an unspoken thing that completely unravels connection. And that thing turned out to be shame. Shame is easily understood as the fear of disconnection: "Is there something about me that, if other people see it or know it, I won't be worthy of connection?" The thing that underpinned this shame was "I'm not good enough," "I'm not thin enough, rich enough, smart enough, promoted enough." And the thing that underpinned that was excruciating vulnerability — this idea that, in order for connection to happen, we have to allow ourselves to be seen, really seen. So I divided my research participants into two main groups: people who truly have a sense of worthiness — a strong sense of love and belonging — and people who struggle for it, who are always wondering if they're good enough. And there was only one variable that separated the people who have a strong sense of love and belonging and the people who really struggle for it. And that was, the people who have a strong sense of love and belonging BELIEVE they're worthy of love and belonging. That's it. They believe they're worthy. The folks who held back, who struggled, were always questioning their worthiness. The people who lived "Wholeheartedly" had a few things in common: courage, compassion, and connection. But most importantly, they embraced vulnerability. They believed that what made them vulnerable made them beautiful. They didn't talk about vulnerability being comfortable, nor did they talk about it being excruciating — they just talked about it as being necessary. They talked about the willingness to say "I love you" first, the willingness to do something where there are no guarantees, the willingness to breathe through waiting for the doctor to call after your mammogram. They were willing to invest in a relationship that may or may not work out. They thought this was fundamental. We live in a vulnerable world. And one of the ways we deal with that is we numb vulnerability. But the problem is — and I learned this from the research — you cannot selectively numb emotion. You can't say, "Here's the bad stuff. Here's vulnerability, here's grief, here's shame, here's fear, here's disappointment. I don't want to feel these. I'm going to have a couple of beers and a banana nut muffin." (Laughter) You cannot numb those hard feelings without numbing the other affects, our emotions. You cannot selectively numb. So when we numb those, we numb joy, we numb gratitude, we numb happiness. And then we are miserable, and we are looking for purpose and meaning, and then we feel vulnerable, so then we have a couple of beers and a banana nut muffin. And it becomes a dangerous cycle. There's another way. To let ourselves be deeply seen, vulnerably seen; to love with our whole hearts, even though there's no guarantee — and that's really hard; to practice gratitude and joy in those moments of terror, when we're wondering, "Can I love you this much? Can I believe in this passionately?"; and to believe that we are enough. Because when we work from a place that says, "I'm enough," then we stop screaming and start listening, we're kinder and gentler to the people around us, and we're kinder and gentler to ourselves. Thank you.

## 57. 3 STEPS TO TURN EVERYDAY GET-TOGETHERS INTO TRANSFORMATIVE GATHERINGS | PRIYA PARKER

We spend so much of our lives gathering — in staff meetings, dinner parties, birthday celebrations, industry conferences, family reunions, and community town halls. Yet, so many of our gatherings are dull, awkward, transactional, and forgettable. We rely on stale etiquette, rigid agendas, and superficial small talk, leaving people feeling drained rather than connected. Why? Because we focus almost entirely on the mechanics of the gathering — the food, the venue, the PowerPoint slides, the playlist — while ignoring the human heart of gathering: intentionality, connection, and purpose. As a professional facilitator and author of "The Art of Gathering," I believe we can turn everyday get-togethers into transformative, memorable experiences by following three powerful steps. Step one: Commit to a bold, specific purpose. Most gatherings fail because they have a vague or general purpose. "A monthly team sync," "a birthday dinner," or "a networking mixer" are not purposes; they are categories. Ask yourself: WHY are we bringing these specific people together at this specific moment? What is the core need, challenge, or opportunity? A specific purpose guides every decision — who to invite, where to host, and what rules to set. Step two: Exercise cause-driven authority and ditch "chill" hosting. Hosts often think that being a good host means stepping back, being passive, and letting guests "do whatever they want." But passive hosting creates chaos and awkwardness. Good hosting requires protective, generous authority. Protect your guests from conversational hijackers, enforce gentle ground rules (like banning phones at the dinner table), and design structured icebreakers that equalize power dynamics so everyone feels seen and included. Step three: Create a memorable closing. Endings matter immensely. Don't let your gathering fizzle out with logistics, cleanup talk, or people quietly slipping out the back door. End with a meaningful reflection, a shared toast, a final story, or a collective commitment that honors the time spent together. Gathering is a uniquely human art form. When we gather with purpose and courage, we build communities that heal, inspire, and transform us. Thank you.

## 58. THE POWER OF LITTLE CELEBRATIONS IN OUR LIVES | EMILE WAROT | TEDXYOUTH@LFNY

We live in a culture obsessed with giant milestones. We wait for the massive promotions, graduation ceremonies, wedding days, buying a first home, or winning a major championship before we allow ourselves to feel proud, throw a party, and celebrate. We spend months, sometimes years, grinding through stress, exhaustion, and routine, holding our breath until we cross some distant, major finish line. But life does not happen only at the major finish lines. Life happens in the everyday middle — in the quiet, mundane, unglamorous Tuesdays between those big milestones. When we postpone our joy and gratitude until the "big moments" arrive, we rob ourselves of happiness and make life feel like an endless, exhausting treadmill. The secret to living a vibrant, joyful, and resilient life lies in harnessing the power of little celebrations. What is a little celebration? It is intentionally pausing to acknowledge, honor, and savor the small victories, micro-moments, and everyday magic that happen around us all the time. Finished a tough week at school or work? Celebrate with a favorite cup of tea. Helped a friend solve a problem? Celebrate with a high-five and a genuine smile. Noticed a beautiful sunset on your walk home? Pause for sixty seconds and absorb the beauty. Little celebrations rewire our brain through positive reinforcement. They release dopamine, lower stress hormones, build emotional resilience, and strengthen social bonds with the people we love. They remind us that happiness is not a distant destination we reach at the end of a long journey; happiness is a habit we practice along the way. Stop waiting for the big moments to give yourself permission to feel joyful. Celebrate the small steps, the micro-wins, and the everyday moments. Life is worth celebrating right now. Thank you.

## 59. HOW CULTURE DRIVES BEHAVIOURS | JULIEN S. BOURRELLE | TEDXTRONDHEIM

Culture is the invisible lens through which we view the world, interpret human behavior, communicate, and navigate social interactions. When you grow up in a specific country or society, you absorb cultural norms, values, and behavioral codes so deeply that they become your unquestioned definition of "normal." You assume that the way you communicate, express emotions, build relationships, and view time is simply "human nature." But when you travel, live abroad, or work in multicultural teams, you quickly discover that what is polite, respectful, and normal in one culture can be confusing, rude, or offensive in another. Culture drives behavior in profound ways that we rarely realize until we step outside our own cultural bubble. Consider how different cultures view personal space and social interaction. In Norway, where I live, social norms prioritize autonomy, privacy, and non-intrusion. If you sit next to a stranger on a public bus when other seats are empty, Norwegians will look at you with deep suspicion and discomfort! They view personal space as a sign of respect. However, in many South American or Southern European cultures, sitting next to someone and striking up a friendly conversation is seen as warm, polite, and welcoming. Standing far away is seen as cold and distant. Neither culture is "right" or "wrong"; they are simply operating on different cultural operating systems. Consider communication styles: direct vs. indirect. In direct cultures (like Germany or the Netherlands), honesty is valued above harmony. People say exactly what they mean, critique work directly, and use clear language. In indirect cultures (like Japan or Thailand), harmony is valued above directness. People use subtle cues, body language, and soft phrasing to avoid causing anyone to "lose face." To thrive in an interconnected global world, we must develop cultural intelligence. Cultural intelligence requires three steps: first, awareness of your own cultural biases; second, curiosity and observation without immediate judgment; third, adapting your behavior to connect respectfully across cultural boundaries. Diversity is not just about where people are born; it is about how we learn to understand and appreciate different ways of seeing the world. Thank you.

## 60. THE SCIENCE OF VACATION: IAN COLE AT TEDXORLANDO

In modern work culture, particularly in North America, busyness and overwork have become badges of honor. We brag about working 80-hour weeks, sending emails at midnight, skipping lunch breaks, and leaving paid vacation days unused on the table every single year. We treat rest and leisure as signs of laziness or lack of ambition. We tell ourselves: "I'm too busy to take a vacation. I'll take time off when the project is done, when the quarter ends, or when I retire." But scientific research across neuroscience, medicine, and organizational psychology reveals a striking truth: taking regular vacations is not an indulgence or a luxury; it is a biological, psychological, and professional necessity. Overwork and chronic stress elevate cortisol levels, causing systemic inflammation, cardiovascular disease, depression, anxiety, and severe burnout. The human brain was not designed to operate in a continuous state of high-alert productivity without rest. Neuronal networks require periods of downtime — what neuroscientists call the "Default Mode Network" — to consolidate memories, process complex emotions, spark creative insights, and restore cognitive energy. When you take a real vacation — disconnecting completely from work emails, Slack messages, and project deadlines — your body and brain undergo profound physiological recovery. Blood pressure drops, sleep quality improves, stress hormones plummet, and immune function rebounds. Furthermore, research shows that workers who take regular vacations are actually MORE productive, more creative, and more engaged when they return to work. They receive higher performance reviews and are less likely to quit their jobs compared to workaholics who never take time off. Vacation is an investment in your health, your longevity, your relationships, and your long-term professional success. Stop glorifying overwork. Turn on your out-of-office auto-responder, step away from your desk, and take the vacation you have earned. Your body, mind, and career will thank you. Thank you.
