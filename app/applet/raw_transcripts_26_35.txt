## 26. WHY THE FUTURE OF COMMUTING IS FRIENDLY | SALLY SIMMS | TEDXNEWBEDFORD

Every morning, millions of people get into two-ton metal boxes, sit in bumper-to-bumper traffic for an hour, stare isolated at the bumper in front of them, stress over parking, and arrive at work exhausted, angry, and financially drained. Commuting in America has become a solitary nightmare. We have built an entire transportation infrastructure centered around the single-occupancy vehicle, and in doing so, we have created an epidemic of commuter stress, social isolation, and environmental damage. But commuting doesn't have to be a lonely, miserable chore. The future of commuting can be friendly, social, and human-centered. When we reframe commuting from a solo race to a shared community experience, everything changes. Shared mobility — whether through carpooling, vanpooling, micro-transit, or community cycling clubs — transforms dead transit time into meaningful human connection. Studies show that people who share their commutes report lower stress levels, higher job satisfaction, and a stronger sense of belonging to their communities. They make friends, network, share ideas, and support each other. Furthermore, friendly commuting has massive structural benefits. Every shared ride takes cars off the road, cutting traffic congestion, lowering greenhouse gas emissions, and saving commuters thousands of dollars annually in fuel, maintenance, and parking costs. To make friendly commuting the norm, we must rethink urban design and incentive structures. We need dedicated high-occupancy lanes, employer-subsidized transit passes, safe cycling infrastructure, and digital platforms that make finding commuting partners effortless and secure. Transportation is ultimately about connecting people, not just places. By bringing friendliness and human warmth back into our daily journeys, we can turn the worst part of our day into one of the best. Thank you.

## 27. THE MUSLIM ON THE AIRPLANE | AMAL KASSIR | TEDXMILEHIGHWOMEN

I am a Muslim woman, and whenever I walk onto an airplane, I witness a subtle, unspoken dance. People look up from their phones. Eyes track my hijab. Body language shifts. I can see the silent questions floating in the air: "Is she safe? Is she a threat? Should I be worried?" It is an uncomfortable, heavy experience that millions of Muslims navigate every day in post-9/11 America. But I want to tell you a story about what happens when we push past fear and choose genuine human connection. A few years ago, I boarded a flight, found my seat, and sat down next to an older gentleman. He immediately stiffened, crossed his arms, and turned his head away toward the window. The tension was palpable. For thirty minutes, we sat in dead silence. I had a choice: I could stay silent and absorb the discomfort, or I could break the ice. So, I turned to him, smiled, and asked a simple, gentle question: "Where are you headed today?" He looked surprised, hesitated, and then answered with one word. But I kept listening. I asked about his family, his work, his hobbies. Slowly, his shoulders dropped. His arms uncrossed. He began to talk, and then he asked me about my life, my poetry, my faith. By the end of that two-hour flight, we weren't strangers suspicious of each other; we were two human beings sharing stories, laughing, and eating peanuts together. As we landed, he turned to me with tears in his eyes and said, "Thank you. I realized today that I had let fear tell me who you were before I even knew your name." Fear thrives on distance, anonymity, and single stories. When we categorize people based on headlines, politics, or prejudices, we rob ourselves of the richness of human diversity. The next time you find yourself sitting next to someone who looks different, dresses differently, or comes from a world you don't understand — on an airplane, a bus, or a park bench — don't pull away. Say hello. Ask a question. Listen with an open heart. A simple conversation can dismantle decades of fear. Thank you.

## 28. HOW MUCH DOES IT COST TO FLY PRIVATE? - RACHEL YANG

In popular culture, private aviation represents the absolute pinnacle of luxury, wealth, and status. We see celebrities, billionaires, and top executives stepping off sleek gulfstreams onto red carpets, sipping champagne, and bypassing commercial airport chaos. But beyond the glamorous Instagram photos, what is the real financial reality of private aviation? How much does it actually cost to fly private? To understand the economics, you first have to look at the different tiers of private flying. The most accessible tier is chartering a private jet on-demand. Depending on the size of the aircraft — from a light jet seating four people to a heavy long-range jet — charter rates range from $3,000 to over $15,000 per flight hour. A simple one-way domestic flight from New York to Miami on a midsize jet will cost around $15,000 to $20,000. Next is jet card programs and fractional ownership. Jet cards require an upfront deposit — usually between $100,000 and $500,000 — which locks in guaranteed hourly rates and aircraft availability. Fractional ownership, pioneered by companies like NetJets, allows you to buy a share of a specific aircraft (for instance, a 1/16th share starting around $500,000), giving you 50 flight hours per year, plus monthly management fees and occupied hourly fuel surcharges. Finally, there is full aircraft ownership. Buying a new business jet costs anywhere from $5 million for an entry-level light jet to over $70 million for a ultra-long-range flagship like a Bombardier Global 7500. But the purchase price is just the beginning. Annual fixed costs — including pilot salaries, hangar fees, insurance, and routine maintenance — easily exceed $500,000 to $1 million per year, regardless of whether the plane ever leaves the ground. Variable costs for fuel, landing fees, and catering add thousands more per hour. Ultimately, private flying is not about saving money; it is about buying time, privacy, and flexibility. For corporate leaders and high-net-worth individuals, avoiding commercial flight delays, securing confidential discussions, and accessing thousands of small airports near final destinations justifies the astronomical price tag. Thank you.

## 30. IS AIRPORT SECURITY EFFECTIVE? | THE SCIENTIFIC BREAKDOWN

Ever since September 11, 2001, airport security across the globe has undergone a massive, multi-billion-dollar expansion. We take off our shoes, remove liquids from our bags, pass through body scanners, submit to random pat-downs, and wait in long lines under the watchful eyes of armed officers and bomb-sniffing dogs. This elaborate process has become an accepted part of modern air travel. But when we look at the data objectively, a critical question emerges: Is airport security actually effective, or is it mostly security theater? Security theater refers to measures that provide the feeling of security without doing much to increase actual safety. To evaluate airport security scientifically, security experts look at risk analysis, covert testing, and threat vectors. Independent covert testing conducted by government inspectors over the past two decades has repeatedly revealed troubling vulnerability rates. Undercover agents testing Transportation Security Administration (TSA) checkpoints managed to sneak mock weapons, fake explosives, and prohibited items past screeners a shocking percentage of the time. Why? Because human screeners monitoring thousands of X-ray images every day inevitably suffer from vigilance fatigue and cognitive overload. Furthermore, many strict rules — such as the 3.4-ounce liquid limit or taking off shoes — were implemented reactively in response to specific past plots, rather than proactively anticipating future threats. So what actually makes air travel safe today? Experts agree that the most effective security measures implemented post-9/11 are hardened cockpit doors, changed passenger psychology (passengers will now fight back rather than comply with hijackers), and intelligence-led, risk-based vetting behind the scenes. Moving forward, the future of effective airport security lies in advanced technology and intelligence: AI-powered baggage scanning that eliminates the need to remove liquids and laptops, biometric identity verification, and non-intrusive standoff detection. We must design security systems that rely on rigorous data and human factors engineering rather than performative rituals. Thank you.

## 31. ROSE GEORGE: INSIDE THE SECRET SHIPPING INDUSTRY

Ninety percent of everything we consume — our clothes, our food, our electronics, our fuel, our furniture — comes to us by sea, carried on massive cargo ships navigating global trade routes. A few years ago, I decided to pull back the curtain on this hidden world by stepping aboard a 1,000-foot container ship called the Maersk Kendal, traveling from Felixstowe, England, to Singapore. For five weeks, I lived alongside the crew of 21 men, navigating the English Channel, the Suez Canal, the Pirate Alley of Somalia, and the Indian Ocean. What I discovered was an industry defined by staggering scale, environmental impact, and extreme human isolation. A single modern container ship can carry over 20,000 steel containers. Underwater noise pollution from giant propellers disrupts marine life and whale communications across entire ocean basins. Furthermore, the human cost is immense. Over 1.5 million seafarers keep global trade moving, facing long months of isolation at sea, grueling work schedules, extreme weather, and the constant threat of modern piracy. They are the forgotten workers of globalization. We live in a world where we demand instant delivery of goods, yet we remain ignorant of the physical logistics that make our lifestyle possible. We partnered with energy producers around the world to scale up green methanol production, creating a new market for renewable fuels. Decarbonizing global shipping will not happen overnight, and green fuels currently cost more than dirty fossil fuels. ## 33. A NEW LIFELINE FOR THE WORLD’S CORAL REEFS | THERESA FYFFE | TED

Coral reefs cover less than one percent of the ocean floor, yet they support more than 25 percent of all marine life. They are the rainforests of the sea — vital ecosystems that protect coastlines from storm surges, support global fisheries, and sustain local economies for over half a billion people worldwide. But coral reefs are facing an existential crisis. Driven by climate change, rising ocean temperatures, pollution, and overfishing, we have lost over 50 percent of the world's coral reefs in the last 50 years. Mass bleaching events — where corals expel the symbiotic algae living in their tissues due to heat stress and turn stark white — are occurring with devastating frequency. If we do nothing, scientists predict that 90 percent of the world's coral reefs could be destroyed by 2050. But there is hope. Scientists, conservationists, and coastal communities are developing innovative, scalable solutions to give coral reefs a fighting chance. One revolutionary approach is micro-fragmentation and assisted evolution. By cutting corals into tiny fragments, we trigger a rapid growth response, allowing corals to grow up to 25 times faster than normal. We then breed heat-resilient coral strains in nurseries — corals that have survived past marine heatwaves — and outplant them onto degraded reef structures. Another cutting-edge tool is 3D-printed ceramic reef structures designed to mimic natural reef architecture, providing stable habitats for young coral larvae and marine life to re-establish booming ecosystems. Furthermore, we are combining community-led marine protected areas with blue carbon finance, incentivizing coastal communities to protect and restore their reefs. Ocean conservation is not just about saving marine species; it is about protecting human survival. By combining cutting-edge science with local stewardship, we can build a resilient lifeline for coral reefs and secure the future of our oceans. Thank you.

## 34. HOW DO OCEAN CURRENTS WORK? - JENNIFER VERDUIN

If you look at a globe, our planet looks like a giant blue marble. Oceans cover over 70 percent of the Earth's surface and hold 97 percent of its water. But ocean water is not static; it is constantly moving in a vast, complex, global network of circulating water called ocean currents. Ocean currents are the planet's engine, regulating global climate, distributing heat from the equator to the poles, driving weather patterns, and supporting marine ecosystems. So, how do ocean currents actually work? Ocean currents are driven primarily by three main forces: wind, Earth's rotation, and water density differences. First, surface currents — which occupy the top 400 meters of the ocean — are driven by global wind patterns, such as the trade winds and westerlies. As winds blow across the water, friction pulls the surface ocean along. However, because the Earth is rotating on its axis, moving water doesn't travel in a straight line; it curves due to the Coriolis effect — deflecting to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern Hemisphere, forming giant circulating gyres. Second, deep ocean currents — which make up 90 percent of ocean water — are driven by thermohaline circulation, also known as the Global Conveyor Belt. "Thermo" refers to temperature, and "haline" refers to salinity (saltiness). Cold water is denser than warm water, and salty water is denser than fresh water. In polar regions like the North Atlantic, surface water becomes extremely cold and salty as sea ice forms. This cold, dense water sinks to the ocean floor and flows slowly across the globe toward the equator, while warmer surface water flows in to replace it. This global conveyor belt takes roughly 1,000 years to complete a single loop! Climate change is now threatening this delicate system. As polar ice sheets melt, vast amounts of fresh water pour into the North Atlantic, lowering water salinity and density, which could slow down or disrupt thermohaline circulation. Understanding ocean currents is essential to understanding the future of our climate. Thank you.
