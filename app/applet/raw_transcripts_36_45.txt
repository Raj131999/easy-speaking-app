## 36. 3 WAYS TO UPGRADE DEMOCRACY FOR THE 21ST CENTURY | MAX RASHBROOKE

Democracy across the world is facing a profound crisis of legitimacy. Citizens feel disconnected from political institutions, voter turnout is declining, polarization is skyrocketing, and trust in elected officials is at historic lows. Many people feel that our political systems are rigged in favor of wealthy donors, corporate lobbyists, and political elites, leaving ordinary citizens feeling powerless. The problem is that we are trying to run 21st-century societies on an 18th-century operating system. Representative democracy — where citizens cast a ballot once every three or four years and then hand over all decision-making power to professional politicians — is no longer enough to meet the demands of an informed, connected citizenry. To save democracy, we must upgrade it. Here are three powerful ways to modernize democracy for the 21st century. First, participatory budgeting. Instead of politicians deciding how public tax dollars are spent behind closed doors, participatory budgeting allows ordinary citizens to directly deliberate, propose, and vote on how a portion of the city or municipal budget is allocated. Pioneered in Porto Alegre, Brazil, and now used in hundreds of cities worldwide, participatory budgeting builds civic trust, improves infrastructure, and directs resources to marginalized communities. Second, citizens' assemblies selected by sortition. Sortition is the ancient Greek practice of choosing public decision-makers by random lottery rather than election — much like jury duty. A citizens' assembly brings together a representative cross-section of society — rich and poor, young and old, urban and rural — to learn from experts, deliberate respectfully, and make policy recommendations on contentious issues like climate change or constitutional reform. Free from re-election pressures and partisan toxicity, citizens' assemblies consistently produce thoughtful, common-good solutions. Third, digital direct democracy and liquid democracy. Using secure digital platforms like Taiwan's vTaiwan system, citizens can continuously debate legislation, propose petitions, and vote directly on policy issues or delegate their vote to trusted proxy experts on specific topics. Upgrading democracy is not about replacing representative government, but enriching it with citizen power. Democracy is not a spectator sport; it is a muscle that must be exercised every day. Thank you.

## 37. ERIC LIU: WHY ORDINARY PEOPLE NEED TO UNDERSTAND POWER

We live in an age of profound cynicism about politics and power. Many people look at the world around them — growing economic inequality, systemic injustice, political dysfunction — and feel completely powerless. They believe that power belongs exclusively to billionaires, politicians, corporate CEOs, and media moguls, and that ordinary citizens have no meaningful influence over how society is run. But power is not a mysterious force reserved for the elite. Power is simply the capacity to make others do what you would have them do. It is the ability to shape reality. And the fundamental problem in our democracy today is a widespread literacy gap when it comes to power. Too many good people don't understand how power works, where it comes from, or how to organize it, while those who do understand power use it to advance their own self-interest. If we want a healthy, equitable democracy, we must teach civic power literacy to everyone. Power flows from six primary sources: physical force, wealth, state action, social norms, ideas, and numbers. In a democracy, the ultimate source of power is numbers — organized people. When ordinary people come together, understand their shared interests, and organize their collective energy, they can challenge wealth, state action, and entrenched norms. Think about every major social movement in history — the civil rights movement, the women's suffrage movement, labor rights, environmental protection. None of these changes began in the halls of Congress; they began when ordinary citizens built power from the bottom up. Understanding power requires three things: reading power (analyzing who has power, how they got it, and how they maintain it), writing power (organizing coalitions, creating new narratives, and taking strategic action), and sharing power (ensuring that power is distributed democratically rather than concentrated at the top). Power is not inherently dirty or evil; power is neutral. Power is like water — it flows, and whoever directs the channel shapes the landscape. It is time for ordinary citizens to claim their power. Thank you.

## 38. A CRASH COURSE IN MAKING POLITICAL CHANGE | KATIE FAHEY | TED

In 2016, I was a 26-year-old recycling coordinator from Michigan with zero political experience. Like millions of Americans, I was exhausted by political polarization and frustrated that politicians seemed to care more about keeping their seats than serving the public. In Michigan, our legislative districts were so severely gerrymandered by politicians behind closed doors that elections were essentially decided before a single vote was cast. Politicians were choosing their voters, rather than voters choosing their politicians. On the morning after the 2016 election, I decided to do something about it. I posted a simple, 14-word status update on Facebook: "I want to take on gerrymandering in Michigan. If you're interested in helping, let me know." I didn't have a donor network, a political action committee, or a grand strategic plan. But that single post struck a nerve. Thousands of ordinary citizens responded — teachers, engineers, retirees, students, Republicans, Democrats, and Independents. Together, we formed a nonpartisan, grass-roots organization called "Voters Not Politicians." Over the next two years, an army of 10,000 volunteers collected over 425,000 physical signatures in rain, snow, and heat across all 83 counties in Michigan to get a constitutional amendment on the state ballot. We faced immense opposition from powerful political establishments, corporate lobbyists, and lawsuits aimed at knocking us off the ballot. But we persevered. In November 2018, Proposal 2 passed with 61 percent of the vote, successfully stripping politicians of the power to draw their own district lines and transferring that power to an Independent Citizens Redistricting Commission. Through this incredible journey, I learned three crucial lessons about making political change. First, you don't need permission or elite credentials to lead; you just need the courage to start. Second, focus on common ground rather than partisan identity — people are hungry for fairness. Third, citizen power is real when people step up and do the hard, unglamorous work of democracy. You don't have to wait for a hero to save your community; you can be the change you want to see. Thank you.

## 39. THE VALUE OF TRAVEL | RICK STEVES | TEDXRAINIER

For over forty years, I have made my living encouraging Americans to travel to Europe and beyond. But travel is more than just sightseeing, taking selfie photos in front of famous monuments, or collecting passport stamps. Travel, when done thoughtfully, is a transformative political and personal act. It is a powerful antidote to fear, ethnocentrism, and narrow-mindedness. When we step outside our comfort zones and immerse ourselves in different cultures, we develop what I call a "broader perspective." We realize that the world is filled with good, hard-working, loving people who happen to see the world differently than we do. We learn that God didn't just bless our home country, but loves all of humanity. We learn that other societies have creative, effective solutions to social problems — whether it is healthcare, public transit, drug policy, or urban planning — that we can learn from. Traveling thoughtfully requires us to be "curious rather than judgmental." When you encounter something that seems strange, inefficient, or shocking in another culture, don't immediately declare it wrong or inferior. Ask yourself: "Why do they do it this way? What history, geography, or values shaped this behavior?" When you travel with an open mind, you come home with a valuable gift: the ability to look at your own country from a fresh, critical perspective. You see your nation's great strengths, but you also see its flaws and blind spots more clearly. Travel challenges our national narcissism and replaces it with global citizenship. In an increasingly interconnected and fragile world, we cannot afford to hide behind walls or view other nations with suspicion. Travel equips us to be engaged, empathetic citizens who understand that our security and prosperity are bound up with the rest of the planet. So, pack your bags, leave your expectations at home, and go out to embrace the world. Thank you.

## 40. LEARN TO TRAVEL -- TRAVEL TO LEARN: ROBIN ESROCK AT TEDXVANCOUVER

We live in a world obsessed with safety, routine, and predictable comfort. We spend our lives following prescribed paths: school, job, mortgage, promotions, retirement. We live inside comfortable bubbles, consuming curated media and experiencing the world through screens. But real learning, real growth, and real living happen outside your comfort zone. A few years ago, I was hit by a car while riding my bicycle. It was a terrifying, painful wake-up call. I realized in an instant how fragile life is, and how easily we postpone our dreams for a "someday" that may never arrive. So, I took my insurance settlement, quit my job, packed a single backpack, and set off to travel the world. Over the next decade, I traveled to over 100 countries across seven continents. I swam with piranhas in the Amazon, trekked with mountain gorillas in Uganda, explored active volcanoes, and broke bread with families in remote villages. And through those journeys, I discovered that travel is the ultimate university. When you learn to travel, you travel to learn. What does travel teach you? First, it teaches you resilience and adaptability. When you miss a train in a country where you don't speak the language, lose your luggage, or get lost in a chaotic market, you learn to solve problems calmly and trust your instincts. Second, travel teaches you humility. You realize how vast, diverse, and grand the world is, and how small your personal problems really are. Third, travel teaches you that the world is fundamentally good. Despite the fear and conflict broadcast on 24-hour news channels, the overwhelming majority of people across the globe are kind, welcoming, generous, and eager to help a traveler in need. You don't need a fortune to travel the world. You just need curiosity, courage, and a willingness to trade material possessions for unforgettable life experiences. Don't wait for a tragedy to wake you up. Go out and learn from the world. Thank you.

## 41. CHIMAMANDA NGOZI ADICHIE: THE DANGER OF A SINGLE STORY | TED

I'm a storyteller. And I would like to tell you a few personal stories about what I like to call "the danger of a single story." I grew up on a university campus in eastern Nigeria. My mother was an administrator, my father was a professor. I was an early reader, and what I read were British and American children's books. I read storybooks about white children with blue eyes who played in the snow, ate apples, and talked about the weather. Now, I lived in Nigeria. We didn't have snow, we ate mangoes, and we never talked about the weather because there was no need to. But because all the books I read featured foreign characters, I had become convinced that books, by their very nature, had to have foreigners in them and had to be about things with which I could not personally identify. Things changed when I discovered African literature. Books by authors like Chinua Achebe and Camara Laye saved me from having a single story of what books are. I realized that people like me — girls with skin the color of chocolate, whose kinky hair could not form ponytails — could also exist in literature. Later, when I went to college in the United States at age 19, my American roommate was shocked by me. She asked where I had learned to speak English so well, and was confused when I told her that Nigeria happened to have English as its official language. She asked to listen to what she called my "tribal music," and was thoroughly disappointed when I produced my tape of Mariah Carey. She felt default pity for me as an African. Her default position toward me was a patronizing, well-meaning pity. My roommate had a single story of Africa: a single story of catastrophe, poverty, war, and AIDS. In this single story, there was no possibility of Africans being similar to her in any way, no possibility of feelings more complex than pity, no possibility of a connection as human equals. The single story creates stereotypes, and the problem with stereotypes is not that they are untrue, but that they are incomplete. They make one story become the only story. Power is the ability not just to tell the story of another person, but to make it the definitive story of that person. When we show a people as one thing — as only one thing — over and over again, that is what they become. Stories matter. Many stories matter. Stories have been used to dispossess and to defame, but stories can also be used to empower and to humanize. When we reject the single story, when we realize that there is never a single story about any place or any person, we regain a kind of paradise. Thank you.

## 42. THE SECRET TO THRIVING RURAL COMMUNITIES: TERRY BRUNNER AT TEDXABQ

When people think about economic development and the future of prosperity, almost all the attention goes to booming metropolitan areas: Silicon Valley, New York, Austin, Seattle. Rural communities are often written off as dying relics of the past — places plagued by population loss, economic stagnation, and decaying infrastructure. But rural America is not dead, and it does not have to decline. Rural communities hold immense potential, culture, land, and economic value. The secret to thriving rural communities is not waiting for a massive corporation or government agency to come save them; it is bottom-up, community-driven innovation. To build thriving rural economies, we must focus on three core pillars. First, modern infrastructure — specifically universal high-speed broadband internet. In the 21st century, broadband internet is just as essential to rural survival as electricity and paved roads were in the 20th century. High-speed internet allows rural entrepreneurs to start global businesses, enables remote work, connects students to world-class education, and brings telehealth to remote clinics. Second, investing in local assets and place-based economies. Instead of trying to turn rural towns into mini-cities, communities should build on their unique local strengths: sustainable agriculture, outdoor recreation, eco-tourism, local arts, and renewable energy production. Third, cultivating local leadership and youth retention. We must create pathways for young people to see a future in their hometowns — providing small business seed capital, affordable housing, mentorship, and civic engagement opportunities. Thriving rural communities are essential for a balanced, resilient nation. They feed us, power us, and anchor our cultural heritage. When we invest in rural people and local ideas, rural communities don't just survive; they thrive. Thank you.

## 43. JONAS ELIASSON: HOW TO SOLVE TRAFFIC JAMS

Traffic congestion is one of the most frustrating, wasteful problems of modern urban life. Drivers around the world spend billions of hours stuck in gridlock every year, wasting time, burning fuel, polluting the air, and raising their blood pressure. For decades, city governments have tried to solve traffic jams by expanding road capacity — adding more lanes to highways and building new bypasses. But urban planners have known for a long time that building more roads to solve traffic congestion is like loosening your belt to solve obesity. It doesn't work. It simply attracts more cars, creating even worse gridlock in the long run. So, how do you actually solve traffic jams? In Stockholm, Sweden, we proved that the solution is surprisingly simple, elegant, and effective: congestion pricing. In 2006, Stockholm launched a trial charging a small fee to driving in and out of the inner city during peak rush hours. Before the trial, public debate was fierce. Over 70 percent of the public was strongly opposed to the idea of paying a toll to drive. People thought it was a tax grab, that it wouldn't work, and that it was unfair. But when the system was switched on, something miraculous happened overnight. Traffic volume in the inner city dropped by 20 percent immediately! Queue times plummeted by 30 to 50 percent. Air pollution dropped significantly, and public transit ridership surged. Why did a small fee make such a huge difference? Because traffic congestion is non-linear. When a road is operating at 100 percent capacity, removing just 10 to 15 percent of vehicles moves the road from severe gridlock back into smooth, free-flowing traffic. A small price signal encourages drivers to make small adjustments: taking public transit, carpooling, shifting their commute time by 30 minutes, or skipping unnecessary trips. After seven months of experiencing clear roads, Stockholm held a public referendum, and the majority voted to make congestion pricing permanent. Today, the system is widely popular. You can solve traffic jams, not by building more concrete, but by managing demand intelligently. Thank you.

## 44. REDUCING THE VOLUME OF TRAFFIC JAMS: MBONGENI ZWANGOBANI AT TEDXUNIVERSITYOFLEEDS

Traffic gridlock is a global crisis affecting economic productivity, human health, and urban air quality. In growing cities across the developing world and wealthy nations alike, millions of commuters spend hours every day stuck in motionless lines of single-occupancy vehicles. We often view traffic as an inevitable law of nature — something that just happens to us. But traffic is not a natural disaster; traffic is the collective result of individual human choices. You are not stuck in traffic; you ARE traffic. To reduce the volume of traffic jams, we must change how we design transportation networks and how we incentivize human behavior. First, we must prioritize mass transit and active travel over private car dominance. A single bus can carry 60 people, taking 50 private cars off the road. Dedicated bus rapid transit (BRT) lanes, expanded metro systems, and protected bike networks move exponentially more people per hour than car lanes ever could. Second, corporate work culture reform. The traditional 9-to-5 work schedule forces millions of commuters onto the road at the exact same time. By encouraging flexible work hours, hybrid remote work, and staggered shifts, employers can flatten the peak demand curve and eliminate rush-hour gridlock. Third, smart urban land-use design. The "15-minute city" model ensures that housing, workplaces, schools, grocery stores, and parks are integrated within walking or cycling distance, eliminating the need for long daily car commutes altogether. Reducing traffic volume is not about restricting human freedom; it is about providing better, faster, cleaner choices so that cities can move efficiently and breathe freely. Thank you.

## 45. THE COUNTERINTUITIVE SECRET OF LEADERSHIP | JESSICA KRIEGEL | TED

For generations, management books and corporate leadership programs have taught us a specific playbook for what it takes to be a great leader: charismatic authority, decisive command, unwavering confidence, and complete control. We are told that leaders must have all the answers, set rigid strategic targets, and drive their teams relentlessly toward performance metrics. But in today's complex, rapidly changing, volatile world, this traditional, top-down command-and-control leadership model is completely broken. It creates disengaged employees, burnout, fear, and organizational blind spots. The counterintuitive secret of great leadership in the 21st century is not control; it is surrender. It is not having all the answers; it is having the humility to ask the right questions. Great leaders don't manage people; they create the environment where people can manage themselves and thrive. Counterintuitive leadership requires three fundamental shifts. First, vulnerability over invulnerability. When leaders admit what they don't know, acknowledge their mistakes, and share their challenges, it creates psychological safety. Employees feel safe to take risks, speak truth to power, and innovate without fear of punishment. Second, empathy over authority. Traditional leaders focus strictly on output; counterintuitive leaders focus on human connection. When you genuinely care about your team members as whole human beings — listening to their needs, supporting their growth, and honoring their well-being — they respond with unprecedented loyalty, creativity, and performance. Third, alignment over compliance. Instead of forcing compliance through rules and micromanagement, great leaders align people around a shared purpose and mission, and then step back to give them autonomy. When people understand WHY their work matters and have the freedom to execute, they don't need to be managed; they inspire themselves. True power comes from empowering others. Leadership is not about being in charge; it is about taking care of those in your charge. Thank you.
