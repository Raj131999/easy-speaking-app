## 16. WHAT DOCTORS DON'T KNOW ABOUT THE DRUGS THEY PRESCRIBE | BEN GOLDACRE

I'm a doctor, but I kind of got sucked into research accidentally, and now I'm an epidemiologist. And nobody really knows what epidemiology is. Epidemiology is the science of how we know in the real world if something is good for you or bad for you. And it's best understood through examples. And the classic example is the story of a whole load of extra risk of breast cancer that was spotted in women taking hormone replacement therapy, HRT, in 2002. And before that, the conventional wisdom was that HRT reduced the risk of heart disease in women, and doctors were prescribing it to women for that reason, partly. And it turned out that that finding was caused by a classic epidemiological error, which is that women taking HRT in the '70s and '80s and '90s were more likely to be from higher socioeconomic groups. They were wealthier, they ate better, they exercised more, they were less likely to smoke, they were less likely to be overweight. So they had a lower risk of heart disease for reasons that were nothing to do with the HRT. The HRT was just a marker for those other things. And it took a proper trial, a randomized controlled trial, to reveal that HRT actually increased the risk of heart disease. So that's epidemiology. I work on a specific area of epidemiology, which is evidence-based medicine. And evidence-based medicine is very simple. Medicine is full of tricky, complicated, technical questions, like should I give this pill or that pill? But it's also full of very simple, human questions. Like, how do I know if a piece of evidence is real? How do I know if a treatment works? How do I know if a trial is biased? How do I know if doctors are being given all the evidence, or if some evidence is being hidden? Now, if you asked a child, how do you know if a drug works? They would say, well, you give it to some people and see if they get better. But medicine is more complicated than that, because people get better on their own. People get better through the placebo effect. People get better because they have good days and bad days with chronic diseases. So to know if a drug works, you have to do a randomized controlled trial. You take a group of people who are all similar. You split them at random into two groups. One group gets the real drug, one group gets a dummy pill, a placebo, or a treatment that you already know works. And then you compare the two groups at the end, and if the group getting the real drug does better than the group getting the control, then you know the drug works. But there's a problem. What if all the trials that show a drug works get published, but all the trials that show a drug doesn't work, or causes dangerous side effects, get hidden away? Get buried in a filing cabinet somewhere? If that happens, then doctors and patients are being mislead. We are making decisions about which drugs to take, which drugs to prescribe, based on a biased, cherry-picked sample of the evidence. And that is happening today. It is happening in every field of medicine. It's called publication bias. And it's a huge problem. Let me give you an example. Reboxetine is a drug that I prescribed myself as a doctor. It's an antidepressant. And when I prescribed it, I looked at the published evidence, and I saw that reboxetine was better than placebo, and as good as any other antidepressant on the market. But what I didn't know, and what doctors around the world didn't know, was that seven trials had been done comparing reboxetine against placebo. Only one of them was published, and that was the one that showed reboxetine worked. The other six trials, which showed reboxetine was no better than placebo, were never published. They were kept secret by the drug company. Not only that, but when reboxetine was tested against other antidepressants, trials involving nearly 3,000 patients showed that reboxetine was worse than other antidepressants, and caused more side effects. But only data from 507 patients was published, and that data was cherry-picked to make reboxetine look good. So 74 percent of the data on patients in trials for reboxetine was hidden from doctors and patients. This is not an isolated incident. This happens systematically across medicine. When researchers looked at all the trials conducted for antidepressants approved by the FDA over a 15-year period, they found that 38 trials showed positive results, and 36 showed negative or questionable results. Out of the 38 positive trials, 37 were published. Out of the 36 negative trials, 22 were never published at all, and 11 were published in a way that made them look positive. So if you looked at the published literature, you would think that 94 percent of trials for antidepressants were positive. But if you looked at all the trials that were actually conducted, only 51 percent were positive. That means half of the evidence about these drugs was hidden. And this doesn't just happen with antidepressants. It happens with drugs for cancer, drugs for heart disease, drugs for pain, drugs for diabetes. It happens with medical devices. It happens across the board. In fact, systematic reviews show that negative trial results are about twice as likely to be hidden as positive trial results. Think about what that means. It means that the evidence base we use to make decisions in medicine is systematically broken. It means that treatments that don't work are being prescribed to millions of people. It means that treatments that cause harm are being used every day. And it means that we are wasting billions of dollars on drugs that are no better than cheaper alternatives, or no better than dummy pills. Why is this happening? Because nobody is stopping it. The drug companies aren't required by law to publish all trial results. The regulators don't make all trial results public. The medical journals don't demand to see all the trial results before publishing a study. And academic institutions don't enforce rules that require their researchers to publish everything. This is a flaw in the very structure of medicine. It's an ethical failure, it's a scientific failure, and it's a regulatory failure. But it's a failure that can be fixed. We need simple, enforceable rules. Every clinical trial, on any human being, anywhere in the world, must be registered at the start, before the first patient is enrolled. And the results of every trial, positive or negative, must be published within one year of completion. Not just a summary, but the full trial report and the raw, anonymized patient data. Until that happens, we cannot say that medicine is truly evidence-based. We are making choices in the dark. And patients are paying the price. Thank you.

## 18. A DEEPER LOOK INTO OUR JUSTICE SYSTEM | MARK GODSEY | TEDXUCINCINNATI

I spent the first decade of my career as a federal prosecutor in New York City. I was a tough-on-crime guy. I believed in the system. I believed that the police were always right, that prosecutors were always seeking justice, and that if someone was convicted of a crime, it was because they were guilty beyond a reasonable doubt. I was part of the machine. But then I moved to Ohio and became a law professor, and I founded the Ohio Innocence Project. And my eyes were opened to a reality that I had been blind to for ten years. The reality is that innocent people are convicted of crimes they did not commit every single day in the United States. Since the advent of DNA testing, over 3,000 people in America have been exonerated after serving years, sometimes decades, in prison for crimes they had nothing to do with. And DNA is only available in a tiny fraction of cases — less than 10 percent. That means the 3,000 exonerations we know about are just the tip of a massive iceberg. So how does this happen? How does a system that prides itself on due process and justice get it so wrong, so often? It happens because of human psychology and systemic flaws. First, confirmation bias. Once police officers or prosecutors identify a suspect, they subconsciously filter all evidence through that lens. Evidence that fits their theory is highlighted; evidence that contradicts it is ignored or dismissed. They aren't evil people; they are human beings susceptible to psychological blind spots. Second, tunnel vision and cognitive dissonance. When presented with evidence that they might have the wrong person, prosecutors often fight even harder to defend the conviction rather than admit a mistake. Admitting you put an innocent person in prison for 20 years is psychologically devastating, so the brain erects defenses to prevent that realization. Third, junk science and faulty eyewitness identification. Eyewitness memory is not a video recording. It is malleable, fragile, and easily corrupted by suggestive police procedures. Yet, juries believe eyewitnesses more than almost any other form of evidence. And much of the forensic science used in courtrooms for decades — bite mark analysis, hair comparison, arson investigation techniques — has been discredited as unreliable or completely unscientific. Fourth, incentivized informants and coerced confessions. In many cases, convictions rely on "jailhouse snitches" who receive reduced sentences or perks in exchange for testimony, or on false confessions extracted from suspects after hours of high-pressure interrogation techniques designed to break a person's psychological resistance. When I was a prosecutor, I couldn't see these flaws because I was inside the system, protected by a culture of denial. But once you see it, you cannot unsee it. We need systemic reform. We need double-blind eyewitness lineups. We need mandatory recording of all custodial interrogations. We need open-file discovery so prosecutors cannot hide exculpatory evidence. We need independent conviction integrity units in every prosecutor's office. And most importantly, we need a cultural shift from a win-at-all-costs mindset to a truth-seeking mindset. Justice is not about winning cases; it is about getting it right. Thank you.

## 19. THE FUTURE OF TRANSPORTATION | RYAN JANZEN | TEDXTORONTO

Transportation is the vascular system of human civilization. It moves people, goods, and ideas. But for the last century, our transportation systems have been built around a single paradigm: individual vehicles powered by internal combustion engines operating on physical roads. This paradigm has brought us immense freedom, but it has also brought massive inefficiencies, traffic congestion, pollution, and thousands of deaths every day. As we look toward the future, we are on the verge of a fundamental transformation in how we move. The future of transportation is defined by three major technological shifts: electrification, autonomy, and dimensional expansion. First, electrification. Electric vehicles are no longer a niche technology; they are rapidly becoming the dominant mode of road transport. Electrification reduces localized emissions and lowers operating costs, but it also changes vehicle architecture. Electric powertrains are simpler, lighter, and more responsive, allowing for entirely new vehicle form factors, from micro-mobility devices like electric scooters and e-bikes to massive heavy-duty electric trucks and buses. Second, autonomy. Autonomous vehicles will fundamentally reshape urban mobility. By removing the human driver, we eliminate human error, which accounts for over 90 percent of traffic accidents. Autonomous fleets can operate continuously, reducing the need for private car ownership and freeing up vast amounts of urban land currently dedicated to parking. Imagine cities where parking lots are converted into parks, housing, and public spaces because vehicles are constantly in motion, shared by everyone. Third, dimensional expansion. Our current transportation networks are largely two-dimensional — constrained to the surface of the earth. But to truly solve congestion, we must expand into three dimensions: both above and below ground. Underground, high-speed vacuum tube transit systems like Hyperloop promise to connect cities hundreds of miles apart in minutes, traveling at near-supersonic speeds with zero direct emissions. Above ground, urban air mobility — electric vertical takeoff and landing (eVTOL) aircraft — will create a third dimension for short-range passenger transport, turning the sky into a dynamic 3D highway network. However, technology alone will not guarantee a better future. If we simply replace human-driven gasoline cars with autonomous electric cars, we may end up with even more traffic congestion as zero-occupancy vehicles cruise the streets endlessly. We must integrate these technologies into unified, multi-modal transit ecosystems designed around human beings, not vehicles. The future of transportation is not just about moving faster or cleaner; it is about designing cities and societies that are more connected, sustainable, and livable for everyone. Thank you.

## 25. HYPERLOOP: REVOLUTIONISING THE FUTURE OF TRANSPORTATION | JAY WALDER | TEDXGATEWAY
